# Krish Raja Communication and Commercial Operating Manual

## 1. Executive diagnosis

**Most likely mechanism:** this is principally **avoidance of interpersonal exposure under status uncertainty**, compensated for by intellectual performance. The pattern is not “bad at selling.” It is a narrower sequencing problem: when stakes become dyadic and personal, analysis, completeness, correction, preparation, or generosity can substitute for making a testable ask and tolerating the other person’s independent judgment.

The overused strengths are fast inference, verbal precision, technical-commercial synthesis, public authority, and conscientious preparation. These strengths work on stage, in writing, and in diagnostic work because the role, audience attention, and value exchange are pre-authorised. One-to-one self-advocacy is different: it requires Krish to state a preference, create a small burden, risk refusal, and stop talking before certainty is available.

The core deficit is therefore **not capability or honesty**. It is primarily emotional tolerance plus a learned habit of restoring control through explanation. Secondary contributors may include cross-cultural norms, prior professional incentives to be useful, and a mismatch between direct analytical communication and some counterparts’ status needs. The neurodivergent-informed literature should be treated as a useful caution against deficit framing, not an explanation of Krish’s behaviour: communication breakdown can be bidirectional and context-dependent.[cite:8]

**Highest-leverage changes:**

1. **Question, summarize, then offer a hypothesis** before stating a conclusion.
2. **Make explicit, bounded asks** without apology, pre-discounting, or escape clauses.
3. **Use exposure-based repetition**, not more conceptual preparation, to train tolerance for silence, uncertainty, and refusal.

Confidence: moderate. The hypotheses fit the reported pattern, but only live-call recordings and behavioural data can distinguish avoidance from genuine strategic choice, time pressure, counterpart quality, or business-model ambiguity.

## 2. Ranked intervention stack

| Rank | Intervention | Expected impact | Evidence quality | Ease | Misuse risk | Krish relevance |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | Explicit asks plus graduated exposure | Very high | Strong to moderate | High | Low | Very high |
| 2 | Discovery sequence: question, summary, permission, hypothesis, ask | Very high | Moderate to strong | High | Medium | Very high |
| 3 | If-then plans for blurting, correction, apology, and avoidance | High | Strong | High | Low | Very high |
| 4 | Five-minute transcript review with behavioural scorecard | High | Moderate | Medium | Medium | High |
| 5 | Face-saving challenge and repair language | Medium-high | Moderate | High | Low | High |
| 6 | Stakeholder and decision-process mapping | Medium-high | Moderate | Medium | Low | High |
| 7 | Tactical empathy, labels, calibrated questions | Medium | Mixed | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| 8 | Status-performance models | Medium | Practitioner and indirect evidence | Medium | High | Medium |

**Start here:** For 30 days, make **one clean, specific ask every working day**. No amount of positioning work substitutes for collecting evidence that requests are survivable, often welcome, and commercially necessary.

The empirical basis is especially relevant because help-seekers commonly underestimate compliance with direct requests, partly because they underweight the social discomfort potential helpers feel when refusing.[cite:11] Asking questions, especially responsive follow-ups, is also associated with greater interpersonal liking.[cite:9]

## 3. Framework library

### 3.1 Grice’s conversational maxims

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| NAME | Cooperative Principle and conversational maxims |
| DOMAIN | Pragmatics and conversational sequencing |
| SOURCE | H. P. Grice, 1975, “Logic and Conversation” |
| EVIDENCE STRENGTH | Foundational theoretical model; broad influence, not a direct behavioural intervention |
| MECHANISM | People infer meaning partly from expectations of quantity, quality, relevance, and manner. Over-disclosure violates quantity and relevance when it supplies more than the interaction can use. |
| APPLIES WHEN | Explaining, advising, selling, negotiating, and repairing misunderstandings |
| DOES NOT APPLY WHEN | Safety, legal, ethical, or material factual disclosure requires completeness |
| FAILURE MODES | Treating conversational economy as withholding; assuming all cultures interpret implication similarly |
| OPERATIONALISATION | Before speaking, ask: “What is the minimum accurate sentence that moves this conversation one step?” Give that sentence, then invite response |
| KRISH-SPECIFIC USE | Replace a five-minute diagnosis with a 20-second provisional hypothesis followed by one question |
| SOURCES | Grice, 1975; pragmatic theory, primarily conceptual |

### 3.2 Face-work and politeness

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| NAME | Face-work and politeness theory |
| DOMAIN | Status, disagreement, requests |

| EVIDENCE STRENGTH | Strong influence in pragmatics; culturally contingent and criticised for universal claims |

### 3.5 Ladder of inference and left-hand column

### 3.6 Humble Inquiry

|---|---|
| NAME | Humble Inquiry |

| EVIDENCE STRENGTH | Practitioner-academic model; consistent with listening and autonomy-support research |

### 3.7 Conversational receptiveness

### 3.8 Question-asking and liking

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| NAME | Question-asking and interpersonal liking |
| DOMAIN | Rapport and discovery |
| SOURCE | Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson and Gino, 2017, “It Doesn’t Hurt to Ask” |
| EVIDENCE STRENGTH | Moderate to strong across several studies; not a universal prescription |
| MECHANISM | Questions, especially follow-ups, signal responsiveness, which predicts liking. More questions are not inherently better if they feel interrogative or instrumental. |

| OPERATIONALISATION | For the first 10 minutes, ask at least three substantive questions and one follow-up before offering a diagnosis |
| KRISH-SPECIFIC USE | Use follow-ups to learn political and commercial context rather than merely technical facts |
| SOURCES | Huang et al., 2017. Asking more, particularly follow-up, questions was associated with greater liking through perceived responsiveness.[cite:9] |

### 3.9 Motivational interviewing

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| NAME | Motivational interviewing |

### 3.10 Reactance

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| NAME | Psychological reactance |
| DOMAIN | Persuasion, autonomy, resistance |
| SOURCE | Jack Brehm, 1966, *A Theory of Psychological Reactance* |
| EVIDENCE STRENGTH | Strong theoretical and empirical tradition; effects vary by culture, identity, medium, and perceived threat |
| MECHANISM | People resist when they perceive their freedom of choice is being constrained. Controlling language can provoke anger and counterarguing. |
| APPLIES WHEN | Recommendations, change proposals, executive challenge, requests for commitment |
| DOES NOT APPLY WHEN | The counterpart has delegated authority to you, or immediate direction is necessary |
| FAILURE MODES | Over-accommodating autonomy until no clear recommendation remains |
| OPERATIONALISATION | Replace “You need to…” with “My recommendation is X because Y. Does that fit your constraints?” |
| KRISH-SPECIFIC USE | State a view while preserving choice and status |
| SOURCES | Brehm, 1966; a recent meta-analysis found freedom-threatening language reliably increases reactance, while framing effects are less consistent.[cite:13] |

### 3.12 High-quality listening and strategic silence

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| NAME | Active listening, summarising, and pause tolerance |
| DOMAIN | Rapport, discovery, negotiation |
| SOURCE | Rogers and Farson, 1957, active listening; negotiation and counselling research |
| EVIDENCE STRENGTH | Moderate; strong face validity and supporting relational evidence, less precise causal evidence for “strategic silence” |
| MECHANISM | Accurate summaries demonstrate understanding and surface errors. Silence gives the other person time to elaborate, revise, or make an ask without being rescued. |

| KRISH-SPECIFIC USE | Silence is not a prompt to provide more value; it is evidence-gathering space |
| SOURCES | Rogers and Farson, 1957; MI process evidence.[cite:22] |

### 3.17 SPIN Selling

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| NAME | SPIN Selling |
| DOMAIN | Commercial discovery |
| SOURCE | Neil Rackham, 1988, *SPIN Selling* |
| EVIDENCE STRENGTH | Practitioner research based on observational sales analysis; not peer-reviewed causal evidence |
| MECHANISM | Sequence questions from Situation to Problem, Implication, Need-payoff, and commitment. The buyer articulates the consequences and value of solving a problem. |

### 3.19 Challenger Sale

|---|---|
| NAME | Challenger Sale |

## 4. Conflicts and contradictions

| Conflict | Better-supported approach | Context | Decision rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Challenger “teach and take control” vs SPIN/MI restraint | Sequence them: inquire first, then teach | Complex advisory sales | **Teach after you can state the buyer’s problem, stakes, and decision process in their own terms** |
| Direct self-promotion vs warmth/modesty | Direct, factual, relevant claims | US commercial settings, interviews, proposals | State evidence and outcome; do not humblebrag or recite biography |
| Strategic ambiguity vs clarity | Clarity on commitments; ambiguity only on unresolved pathways | Stakeholder alignment | Be exact about price, scope, owner, date, risk, and decision; preserve openness only where options remain real |
| Information withholding vs generosity | Generosity on useful public insight; restraint on unpaid bespoke diagnosis | Expert services | Give enough to demonstrate thinking; do not give the implementation map before qualification or commitment |
| Tactical empathy vs authenticity | Authentic listening, selectively using tactical forms | Tension or hidden concerns | Use labels only when accurate; never mimic or manufacture rapport |
| High-status behaviour vs asking for help | Equal-status requesting | Referrals, advice, access | Ask clearly and specifically; status comes from owning the request, not pretending not to need anything |
| Directness vs face-saving | Face-saving entry plus direct substantive claim | Correction, disagreement, senior stakeholders | Protect autonomy and dignity unless urgency, ethics, safety, or material error demands immediate clarity |
| Adaptation vs masking | Low-cost adaptation | Cross-style interactions | Adapt sequencing, not core judgment, values, or language identity |

### Teach, inquire, or challenge

**Inquire** when the buyer’s stakes, incentives, authority, or internal politics are unknown. Your conclusion may be technically right but commercially irrelevant.

**Teach** when you have a non-obvious, evidence-backed reframe that explains a problem the buyer already recognizes. Keep it short, make it falsifiable, and connect it to their stated stakes.

**Challenge** when a flawed assumption will materially distort a decision, the counterpart has sufficient relationship safety to hear it, and you can state an alternative path. Challenge the assumption, not the person’s competence.

Use this sequence:

1. “What outcome matters most here?”
2. “What makes that difficult now?”
3. “Let me test a hypothesis.”
4. “My reading is that the constraint is X, not Y.”
5. “If that is right, the next decision is Z. Shall we make it?”

## 5. Strategic conversation operating system

### Before

Prepare one page, maximum.

| Item | Required decision |
|---|---|
| Desired outcome | What must happen by the end of this conversation? |
| Relationship outcome | What should this person believe about working with you afterward? |
| Stakeholder map | Messenger, user, recommender, champion, budget owner, economic buyer, final decision-maker |
| Current belief | What do they probably believe now? Mark evidence versus assumption |
| Minimum information | What must you learn before giving a recommendation? |
| First question | What question should precede your conclusion? |
| Clean ask | What exact action, by whom, by when? |
| Truth timing | What is relevant now, later, or not necessary? |

#### Truth-timing test

Before disclosing a conclusion, diagnosis, credential, price rationale, or implementation detail, ask:

1. **True:** Is it accurate to the best of my knowledge?
2. **Relevant:** Does it bear on the decision now?
3. **Useful:** Does sharing it improve the other person’s ability to decide or act?
4. **Necessary:** Is it required now, or is it a way to relieve my own uncertainty?
5. **Timed:** Has the person shown readiness, asked for it, or supplied sufficient context?
6. **Earned:** Has the relationship or commercial exchange earned this depth of analysis?

If true but not relevant, useful, necessary, timed, and earned: **do not say it yet**. This is not dishonesty. It is sequencing.

### During

1. Open with agenda and decision: “By the end, we should know whether there is a real problem worth solving and whether a next step makes sense.”
2. Ask a first question before giving a conclusion.
3. Listen for interests, emotional stakes, incentives, status concerns, hidden objections, and decision constraints.
4. Summarize: “So the commercial risk is X, and the political risk is Y. Have I got that right?”
5. Ask permission: “Would it be useful if I offered a hypothesis?”
6. Offer a 30- to 90-second hypothesis, not a lecture.
7. Convert corrections into questions: “What evidence would change your view on that?”
8. Let pauses run for two breaths after substantive answers and five seconds after an ask.
9. Use proof selectively: one relevant outcome, one method, one implication.
10. Make the ask plainly: “I recommend a 10-day diagnostic. Are you willing to sponsor it?”
11. Stop talking.

#### Overload signals

You are likely overexplaining when you:

- Add a third example after the point has landed.
- Explain price before the buyer has acknowledged value.
- Answer objections the other person has not raised.
- Mention credentials after they have already accepted competence.
- Add “but no pressure,” “only if useful,” or “I completely understand if not” after a valid ask.
- Interrupt to correct a non-material point.
- Feel a strong urge to remove ambiguity immediately.

### After

1. Confirm owner, action, and date.
2. Send a follow-up of no more than five bullets: decision, scope, owner, date, open question.
3. Record one learning point only.
4. Review for five minutes maximum.
5. End review after choosing one next behaviour.

## 6. Script and situation bank

### Opening discovery without diagnosis

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
“Here is what you need to do with AI.”

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Fast inference restores control but may deny the buyer ownership and hide crucial context.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“What decision are you under the most pressure to make about AI this quarter?”  
“What would make the decision defensible to the board?”  
“Let me test a hypothesis based on that.”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I have a view, but I do not want to outrun the context. What is actually stuck?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Before I offer a recommendation, what would a credible outcome need to demonstrate to you and the board?”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“I work with leaders facing AI decisions that are strategically important but operationally under-specified. Is that a live issue for you, or not currently?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“I can already see the whole problem.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the first question.

### Holding back a conclusion

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Reveal the fully formed conclusion to prove value.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Completeness is mistaken for credibility; silence feels like underperforming.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I have an early read. Before I give it, what have you already tried, and what happened?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I think I see a pattern. What am I missing?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“I have a working hypothesis, but I want to pressure-test it against your internal constraints first.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“I may be wrong, but companies in this position often have a decision-system problem rather than an idea problem. Is that familiar?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Your strategy should be…”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After naming that you have a hypothesis.

### Converting an opinion into a question

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
“You are focusing on the wrong thing.”

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Correction triggers status and autonomy defence.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“What would happen if adoption succeeds but no one can explain why this use case outranks the others?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“What does the current plan assume that might not hold?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“How will the board distinguish a promising experiment from a defensible investment?”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“How are you currently deciding which AI initiatives deserve executive attention?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“That is not how enterprise AI works.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the question.

### Selling Mindmaker or senior advisory work

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Explain all products, frameworks, tools, and credentials.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Breadth is used to establish worth, but creates category confusion and free consulting.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“Mindmaker helps leaders convert AI activity into one defensible decision and an execution path. What is the decision you cannot afford to get wrong?”  
“If that is the issue, I recommend a defined diagnostic rather than a broad consulting engagement.”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I help teams turn AI noise into an executive decision. Where is your decision bottleneck?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“You do not need another AI ideation session. You need a board-defensible choice, evidence behind it, and a route to execution.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“I help CEOs and P&L leaders turn AI experimentation into a decision they can defend to a board. Worth a 20-minute comparison against your current approach?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“We do advisory, workshops, cohorts, software, strategy, agents, data…”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the proposed next step and price or scope if asked.

### Explaining value to a CEO or employer

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Deliver a chronological biography.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Credentials feel safer than a direct claim of value.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I am strongest where a technically complex asset needs to become a commercial decision, positioning, revenue plan, and shipped work. For example, I helped contract a $254,000 publisher proof of concept by translating the opportunity into a commercially credible case. In this role, that matters because [their specific decision].”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I turn complex technical possibilities into commercial movement.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“My value is decision compression: I identify the commercial implication, align stakeholders, and turn it into an executable plan.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“My work sits between technical possibility and commercial commitment. I help leaders cross that gap quickly.”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“I have done lots of interesting things across many fields.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After one proof point and one relevance sentence.

### Asking a warm contact for an introduction

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Hint, over-contextualize, or pre-reject yourself.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
The burden belief converts a normal request into a feared imposition.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“Would you be comfortable introducing me to [name]? I would like to discuss [specific relevant issue]. I will send a three-sentence forwardable note. If it is not appropriate, please say so.”

**PEER VERSION**  
“Could you introduce me to [name] for a conversation about [specific topic]?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Would you be willing to make a short introduction to [name]? The relevance is [one sentence].”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“Is [name] the right person to speak with about [specific decision], or is there someone else you would point me to?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Absolutely no worries at all, I know you are busy, and this is probably too much to ask…”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the easy-decline sentence.

### Asking for a referral

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Ask vaguely for “anyone who might be interested.”

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Vagueness reduces the chance of explicit rejection but makes helping costly.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“Do you know one CEO, operating partner, or transformation leader who is accountable for making an AI investment decision this quarter? I would value an introduction if someone specific comes to mind.”

**PEER VERSION**  
“Who is one person in your network with this problem?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Is there one leader facing this decision whom you would be comfortable connecting me with?”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“I am looking for the executive responsible for [decision]. Is that you, or can you direct me?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Please keep me in mind.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the category and one ask.

### Asking for sponsorship

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Present a plan without asking anyone to own it.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Avoidance of a decisive no disguises itself as stakeholder alignment.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“This needs an executive sponsor to cross functions and survive tradeoffs. Are you willing to sponsor the diagnostic and convene the relevant people?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“Will you own getting the right people into the room?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Are you prepared to sponsor this through the decision and protect the time required?”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“If this becomes relevant, who would need to sponsor it internally?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Maybe we could find someone to champion this.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the direct question.

### Asking for a higher fee

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Justify extensively, apologise, or discount before objection.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Price becomes a referendum on personal worth rather than an exchange of value and capacity.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“For this scope, the fee is $X. It includes [three concrete outputs]. If the budget is lower, we can reduce scope to [defined alternative].”

**PEER VERSION**  
“The work is $X for this scope.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“The right commercial structure is $X because the engagement includes decision design, executive alignment, and a usable operating output.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“Before we invest time in scoping, is a budget in the $X to $Y range realistic for a decision engagement?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“I know this may sound high, but I can probably make something work.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the scope-price tradeoff.

### Asking for budget, commitment, or a decision

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
End with “Let me know your thoughts.”

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Vague interest preserves emotional safety but destroys commercial information.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“Based on what we have discussed, do you want to proceed with the diagnostic at $X, decline it, or decide after [specific missing input] by [date]?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“What is the decision, and who owns it?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Are you willing to allocate budget and nominate an executive owner by Friday?”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“If there is a fit, what is the normal route from first conversation to funded work?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Take your time.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the three-option question.

### Asking someone senior for help

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Hide the ask in admiration or background detail.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Status difference amplifies anticipated shame.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I am seeking your judgment on one decision: [question]. A 20-minute conversation would help. If you cannot, a one-line view by email would still be useful.”

**PEER VERSION**  
“Can I get your view on one decision?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“I am asking because you have direct experience with this exact inflection point.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“I know this is unsolicited. Could I ask one specific question about [domain]?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“I hate to bother you.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the bounded request.

### Following up without apology

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
“Sorry to chase” or “sorry to bother you.”

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Existing is framed as a transgression.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“Following up on [decision]. You said [context]. Is this still active, should we schedule the next step, or should I close the loop?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“Is this still live?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Please let me know whether this remains a priority this quarter.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“Closing the loop on this. If AI decision support is not a priority now, I will step back.”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Just bumping this in case it got lost.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the three-option close.

### Challenging a flawed assumption

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
“That assumption is wrong.”

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Correctness becomes more important than counterpart agency and face.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I agree with the goal of moving quickly. I think the assumption that [X] may be carrying too much weight. What evidence would we need to see before committing on that basis?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I may be missing context, but I see a risk in that assumption.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“The decision is reasonable if X is true. I do not think we have verified X yet.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“A common failure point is assuming X before the economics are clear. How are you testing that?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“You are misunderstanding the market.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After proposing the test.

### Hearing an inaccuracy that does not need correction

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Correct immediately.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
An uncorrected error feels like loss of integrity or competence.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
Ask: “Does this inaccuracy change the decision, relationship, safety, ethics, or next action?” If no, let it pass. If relevant later, note it and return when useful.

**PEER VERSION**  
“Small factual point, but it does not alter what we need to decide.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Not material for today. The decision-relevant question is…”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
No correction unless it affects qualification.

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Actually…”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After redirecting to the decision.

### Dealing with a messenger

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Pitch the whole case and hope it travels accurately.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Giving more detail feels like increasing odds of internal advocacy.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“What role will you play in this decision?”  
“Who experiences the problem, who evaluates the solution, who owns budget, and who signs?”  
“What would make this easy for you to carry internally?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“Who else needs to believe this, and what will they care about?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“Before I recommend anything, I need to understand the decision architecture.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“Are you the person accountable for this decision, or the right route to them?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Let me send you a comprehensive deck.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After asking for the stakeholder map.

### Declining a request

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Offer an essay, a vague maybe, or a resentful yes.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Avoidance of disappointing others.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I cannot take this on in the form proposed. I can offer [bounded alternative], or we should leave it there.”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I cannot do that, but I can do this.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“That does not fit the commitment I can make responsibly.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“Thank you for asking. I am not available for that scope.”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“I am so sorry, I wish I could, maybe later…”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After one alternative.

### Setting a boundary

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Explain every historical reason for the boundary.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Over-justification attempts to make refusal unchallengeable.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I can do X by [date]. I cannot add Y without changing scope, time, or fee. Which do you prefer?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I can do one of those, not both.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“To maintain quality, this requires a scope decision.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“This is outside the engagement as currently defined.”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Let me explain why this is difficult for me…”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the tradeoff.

### Emotionally charged personal conversation

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Diagnose, correct facts, or solve too early.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Analysis is used to reduce emotional uncertainty.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“What I hear is that you felt [impact]. Is that right?”  
“My part was [specific behaviour].”  
“What would repair look like now?”  
Then state your own boundary or view.

**PEER VERSION**  
“I want to understand before I defend myself.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
Not applicable outside genuine personal context.

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
Not applicable.

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“That is not what happened.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the summary, until corrected.

### Recovering after interrupting or forcefulness

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Explain why you interrupted.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Self-justification protects identity as a thoughtful person.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I interrupted and pushed past your point. Please finish.”  
After they finish: “Thank you. My response is…”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I cut you off. Go on.”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“I moved too quickly. Please complete your view.”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“I interrupted. Please continue.”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“I only interrupted because I thought…”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
Immediately after the repair.

### Recovering after giving too much analysis

**LIKELY UNHELPFUL INSTINCT**  
Keep explaining because the disclosure has already happened.

**PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM**  
Sunk-cost disclosure and discomfort with silence.

**BETTER SEQUENCE**  
“I have moved too quickly into solution mode. Let me stop. Which part, if any, is most relevant to your situation?”

**PEER VERSION**  
“I overran the context. What matters most here?”

**SENIOR COUNTERPARTY VERSION**  
“I gave a view before fully understanding your constraints. Which assumption should we test first?”

**COLD CONTACT VERSION**  
“I may be ahead of the evidence. Is this a live issue for you?”

**WHAT NOT TO SAY**  
“Let me explain the rest so it makes sense.”

**STOP-TALKING POINT**  
After the recovery question.

## 7. Asking and self-advocacy ladder

### Reusable request formula

> **Context:** “I am working on [specific objective].”  
> **Request:** “Would you be willing to [specific action]?”  
> **Reason:** “It would help because [relevant reason].”  
> **Ease:** “I can make this easy by [forwardable note, short call, draft].”  
> **Choice:** “If it is not appropriate or you do not have capacity, please say so.”

### Reusable self-advocacy formula

> **Evidence:** “I have done [verifiable relevant work].”  
> **Relevance:** “That matters here because [their problem or decision].”  
> **Outcome:** “I can deliver [specific result] through [bounded scope].”  
> **Ask:** “I recommend [commercial or career decision].”

### Exposure ladder

| Level | Request | Feared outcome | Predicted rejection | Observable result | Correct learning |
|---|---|---|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Ask a colleague for a one-line preference | “I am annoying” | 30% | Reply or no reply | A normal request is not a character claim |
| 2 | Ask for a 15-minute advice call | “They will think I am incompetent” | 40% | Yes, no, or alternative | Advice-seeking can signal respect and judgment |
| 3 | Ask for feedback on one offer sentence | “They will dismiss the work” | 40% | Specific feedback or decline | Specificity lowers effort for the helper |
| 4 | Ask a warm contact for an introduction | “I am exploiting the relationship” | 50% | Intro, decline, or no response | A respectful request gives them agency |
| 5 | Ask for a referral to one buyer type | “They will not want to risk reputation” | 55% | Referral or no | Referrals require fit, not universal approval |
| 6 | Ask for a meeting with a named decision-maker | “I do not deserve access” | 60% | Meeting, redirect, no | Access is a commercial variable, not a worth verdict |
| 7 | Ask for personal help with a bounded practical task | “I am a burden” | 60% | Help or decline | Capacity differs from care |
| 8 | Ask a senior person for sponsorship or advocacy | “They will see me as presumptuous” | 65% | Sponsor, advice, decline | Senior people can decide their own boundaries |
| 9 | State a fee without discounting | “They will reject me as overpriced” | 65% | Accept, negotiate, decline | Price tests scope-value fit, not personal value |
| 10 | Ask for budget and a decision date | “I will look pushy” | 70% | Decision path becomes known | Clarity is commercially respectful |
| 11 | Ask for exception or favourable treatment | “They will resent me” | 75% | Yes, no, terms | A respectful exception request is not entitlement |
| 12 | Ask for commitment from an economic buyer | “A direct no will be humiliating” | 75% | Commit, decline, defer with owner/date | A clean no prevents false pipeline |

### Anti-self-rejection rules

- Never write “sorry to bother you” in a commercial follow-up.
- Do not offer a discount before the buyer objects or before you understand budget constraints.
- Do not add more than one easy-decline sentence.
- Do not offer three solutions when one recommended scope is sufficient.
- Do not interpret delayed replies as a rejection without evidence.
- Do not send a second explanation after the request. Send a follow-up only after the agreed or reasonable interval.
- Separate outcome from identity: **a no means this request did not obtain agreement under these conditions. It does not establish your value.**

### Silence and delayed replies

After making an ask:

1. Wait five seconds in live conversation.
2. In writing, wait the agreed date or five working days.
3. Send one follow-up with a three-way close: proceed, defer to a date, or close.
4. Stop after the second follow-up unless an active relationship or explicit process justifies more.

## 8. The cost of the fix

The objective is **not** to make Krish less direct, less analytical, or more conventionally agreeable. Over-correction creates its own commercial and psychological costs.

| Over-correction | Cost |
|---|---|
| Continuous masking | Higher cognitive load, fatigue, reduced spontaneity, and potential identity strain |
| Excessive softening | Expertise becomes hard to locate; recommendations lose force |
| Permanent restraint | Necessary dissent arrives too late or not at all |
| Political adaptation | Can turn into game-playing rather than genuine coordination |
| Excessive warmth signalling | Can lower perceived decisiveness in senior commercial settings |
| Avoidance of challenge | Clients retain flawed assumptions and pay for false agreement |
| Slow sequencing everywhere | Decisions become slow when urgent clarity is needed |
| Suppressed resentment | Emotional leakage later through sharpness, withdrawal, or overcorrection |

### Do not apply strategic restraint when

- Safety, legal exposure, ethics, or material harm requires immediate clarity.
- A factual error materially changes the decision.
- A counterpart explicitly asks for your judgment.
- The decision deadline makes ambiguity more expensive than discomfort.
- A client’s assumption will cause significant financial, reputational, or operational damage.
- A boundary has already been stated and is being breached.
- You are being asked to misrepresent evidence, conceal risk, or endorse something you believe is false.
- A vague “maybe” is being used to avoid accountability for an explicit decision.
- You have already inquired, summarized, and confirmed the relevant context.

Use directness then: concise claim, evidence, implication, recommended action.

## 9. Real-time diagnostic

Use this in under 30 seconds.

| Question | If yes | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Does this point change safety, ethics, a material fact, or today’s decision? | Genuine need for clarity | State it directly and briefly |
| Have I asked a question and summarized their view before giving mine? | If no | Ask one question or summarize |
| Am I explaining because they asked, or because silence feels unsafe? | Anxious overexplaining | Stop and ask, “What is most useful to explore?” |
| Am I correcting a point that does not alter the decision? | Compulsive correction | Let it pass or note it for later |
| Have they acknowledged the problem, cost, urgency, and decision process? | If no | Do not give bespoke solution detail yet |
| Is the next action obvious, but I have not asked for it? | Avoidance of asking | Make the clean ask now |
| Am I minimizing evidence of my value because it feels self-serving? | Weak self-advocacy | Use fact, relevance, outcome |
| Did their reaction change after status, blame, or competence became salient? | Status threat | Acknowledge stake, restore agency, then continue |
| Did I interrupt, overstate, or over-disclose? | Repair needed | One-sentence repair, then ask a question |
| Am I choosing restraint to help them think, or to avoid being judged? | Avoidance disguised as strategy | State the recommendation and ask for decision |

## 10. Thirty-day practice programme

### Daily exercise

One live interaction per working day:

- Ask one substantive follow-up question.
- Summarize one person’s view before disagreeing.
- Make one clean ask, however small.
- Leave one pause of two breaths.
- Delete one apology, disclaimer, discount, or escape clause from a message.

Maximum preparation: five minutes. Maximum review: five minutes.

### Weekly field assignments

| Week | Assignment 1 | Assignment 2 | Assignment 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Make three low-stakes advice or preference requests | Ask two warm contacts for a specific introduction | Run two discovery calls using question-summary-hypothesis sequence |
| 2 | Ask for three meetings with defined buyer profiles | State a fee or price range in two conversations without pre-discounting | Ask one senior person for a bounded piece of help |
| 3 | Ask for two referrals | Challenge one flawed assumption with a face-saving question | Ask one prospect for an explicit next step, owner, and date |
| 4 | Ask for sponsorship, access, or a decision-maker introduction | Present one clear commercial recommendation to an economic buyer | Make one high-stakes ask: fee, commitment, exception, or partnership |

### If-then plans

- If I notice an urge to correct, then I ask: “What leads you to that conclusion?”
- If I notice an urge to explain more than 90 seconds, then I stop and ask: “What part is most relevant?”
- If I write “sorry,” “just,” “maybe,” “no pressure,” or “if useful,” then I delete it unless literal apology or uncertainty is necessary.
- If a call ends without a next step, then I ask: “What is the decision, who owns it, and when will it be made?”
- If I delay outreach to improve materials, then I send one relevant ask before doing further preparation.

### Weekly transcript review

Review one recorded call or message thread. Score only observable behaviours.

| Measure | Weekly target |
|---|---:|
| Substantive questions before advice | 3+ per discovery conversation |
| Summaries before disagreement | 1+ per significant disagreement |
| Explicit asks | 5 in week 1, 7 in week 2, 9 in week 3, 10 in week 4 |
| Pauses tolerated | 1+ per important call |
| Interruptions | Downward trend, not zero by force |
| Initial monologue duration | Under 90 seconds unless invited |
| Escape clauses removed | 5+ per week |
| Decision-makers identified | Every live qualified opportunity |
| Explicit next steps | 80%+ of serious commercial conversations |
| Repairs attempted | 100% when you notice a real rupture |

### Five-minute review template

1. What was the actual ask?
2. What did I predict would happen?
3. What happened observably?
4. What behaviour helped or hindered?
5. What one move will I repeat or change next time?

### Weekly review question

> Where did preparation replace exposure this week, and what direct action will replace it next week?

### Success criteria after 30 days

Success is not universal approval, reduced discomfort, or a fixed revenue result. Success is:

- 30 or more explicit asks.
- At least 12 asks that involve genuine commercial, reputational, or status exposure.
- A documented stakeholder map for every serious opportunity.
- A clear next step, owner, and date in at least 80% of qualified conversations.
- Fewer than two uninvited explanations longer than 90 seconds per reviewed call.
- Evidence that Krish can state price, value, and a recommendation without apology or pre-discounting.
- At least three instances of repair after interruption, forcefulness, or premature disclosure.
